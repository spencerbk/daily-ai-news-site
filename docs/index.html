  • Three LLM inflection points in twelve months
    simonwillison · 2026-11-24 23:02 UTC

    I gave a keynote at the AI Engineer Code Summit a couple of weeks ago, constructed around the idea that there have been three significant inflection points in the abilities of LLMs and coding agents in the past twelve months. Here's the video of the talk. In this post I'm going to pull out the major inflection point theme and interrogate it more deeply. Coding agents in November 2025 Today, November 24th 2026, is the one year anniversary of the release of Claude Opus 4.5. With hindsight, this was a consequential model. In the Opus 4.5 announcement, Anthropic said: Testers noted that Claude Opus 4.5 handles ambiguity and reasons about tradeoffs without hand-holding. They told us that, when pointed at a complex, multi-system bug, Opus 4.5 figures out the fix. They said that tasks that were near-impossible for Sonnet 4.5 just a few weeks ago are now within reach. This held up. Claude Code was 9 months old at that point, having been introduced alongside Claude 3.7 Sonnet on February 24th, 2025. Opus 4.5 was the first model I had used with Claude Code where the default result was generally the right thing. I hadn't been using Claude Code as a daily driver because it didn't feel reliable enough - I had to correct it often enough that it didn't feel like a clear accelerant. The new Opus changed that. For most of the coding problems I faced on a daily basis, Opus would get them right. It was clearly faster than working through the problems myself. Also in November, OpenAI released GPT-5.1 Instant, GPT-5.1 Thinking, GPT-5.1-Codex, and GPT-5.1-Codex-Max. These had a similar effect on their Codex coding agent: it became reliably useful for many of my coding tasks. The timing was convenient, because the December holidays were upon us. A lot of people, myself included, spent December tinkering with these new tools to see what they could do. By January we were ready to use them for our actual work. Fable class models in June/July We got our first hint of Claude Fable in April, when Anthropic announced Claude Mythos and said that it was too dangerous to release, due to its improved cybersecurity capabilities. Mythos was a model that could not just find security vulnerabilities, it could also figure out how to exploit them. In June we got to see how good that model was for ourselves, with the release of Claude Fable 5 - a defanged Mythos, that would solve most problems but would actively block you from using it for security work. After three glorious days... the US government took it away from us. Fable 5 was placed under export controls that effectively banned the model. We wouldn't get it back until July 1st. For me, Fable was an inflection point because it introduced us to what I now think of as Fable class models. My definition of these: In June I wrote about how Claude Fable is relentlessly proactive, after I asked it to help fix a display glitch in Safari and it spun up its own pyobjc-framework-Quartz screenshot mechanism and a CORS-enabled localhost logging server to help resolve the bug. A more recent example of the power of Fable class models is my mysterious beep finder from a couple of weeks ago. I gave Opus 5.5 and then Fable 5.2 access to five WebAudio microphones in a room full of boxes and it was able to triangulate the source of an infuriating mysterious beep to within about 40cm. Give it a goal, give it the right tools, and watch it cook. I'd currently class Claude Fable 5, 5.1, 5.2 and Claude Opus 5.5 as Fable class (I think of Opus 5.5 as "cheap Fable"). GPT-6 Astra got there for me too, and I think the new Gemini 4 Neon may count as well. These now represent the baseline for what I expect from a frontier model. 3D modeling and game development in September/October Something interesting happened with frontier LLMs over just the past four months: they got really good at 3D modeling. Set them up with Blender or ask them to write Three.js code and they'll produce credible models of almost anything you ask for. I first noticed this with Claude Fable 5, but it really kicked into gear with Opus 5.5, GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, and most recently the Gemini 4 family. Here's Gemini 4 Neon's attempt at "Make a three js animation where yonkers stands up as a big mech (cool stand up motion) and stomps around manhattan and queens and Brooklyn, use openstreetmap mapping data", for example. 3D spatial awareness is crucial if you want to use vision LLMs for robotics. I don't know if 3D modeling capabilities are a side-effect of the labs focusing on that or vice-versa. Back in August I experimented with having Fable 5 build me a raccoon heist game. It had 3D graphics, looked like a game, and played really poorly. GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra did a much better job but the game itself was still pretty dull after the first 90 seconds of play. I was somewhat relieved that designing a game that was fun appeared to remain a uniquely human pursuit. I even used that in a talk in September to reassure people that us humans still had a role to play! Then, I tried telling Claude Opus 5.5 "This game isn't fun enough yet. Make it a LOT more fun." And it worked. My Command & Conquer-style RTS went from boring to deeply entertaining overnight, after Opus spent 12 hours playtesting new features and rebalancing the gameplay. Opus 5.5 was the first model I'd used that appeared to understand fun. I've since worked on several little game projects where prompts like "Think hard about how to make sure it is both fun and beautiful" produced results that kept me engaged for significant amounts of time. Opus 5.5 came out on September 22nd, 2026. I've been to two different conferences since then where people showed me game projects they'd been working on that really did feel like proper, worthwhile games. The indie games scene has descended into a full-blown culture war over AI-assisted programming. A lot of people are calling GTA VI the last pre-generative AI blockbuster. It feels like another inflection point to me. Is Fable 5.2 an inflection point too? I'm still trying to decide if Fable 5.2 represents a new inflection point, or just another incremental improvement. I'm getting so much done with that model right now! It seems to have taste that almost exactly matches my own. I have been burning through all sorts of gnarly problems that I've been thinking about (or procrastinating on) for several years now. The release notes for Datasette 1.0a42, 1.0a43, and 1.0a44 - plus LLM 0.37, 0.37.1, 0.37.2, 0.37.3, and 0.38 - all in the last three weeks, represent a flood of fixes, features and improvements. If this does represent an inflection point, the key feature seems to be that if you can imagine and then clearly describe a piece of software, Fable can build it. That's a wild thing to say, but it matches my experience over the past few weeks. Software development has transformed beyond recognition in the last year.     GPT 6.2 Astra Solves a Historical Mystery Here's an interesting case-study in using GPT-6.2 Astra for historical research, from history professor Mark Humphries: With only minimal prompting, GPT-6.2 Astra was able to autonomously identify the creator of an anonymous 1808 sketchbook held at Yale’s Beinecke Library. It did so extremely quickly (in just 22 minutes), using publicly available primary and secondary sources. To do so, it discovered a little known source in a paper archive, predicted that it would prove its hypothesis if examined and, in the end, it did. Astra found a diary reference reference to a visit by the vicomte de Chabot dated five days before a date mentioned in the sketchbook at a nearby river, and suggested that Chabot's journal of his travels (only available by order from Cleveland State University) might confirm that the sketchbook was his work. Are historians due for a round of Deep Blue? Mark thinks it will be mild: But if the experience of mathematics is any guide, we can also anticipate that sometime next spring or summer we’ll face a sort of crisis of confidence as we struggle to understand how to respond to agentic systems that can do things human historians cannot. To be fair, that crisis will almost certainly be less profound for us than it is for math. The problem in mathematics, of course, is that solving hard math problems was the whole game. Historians, on the other hand, constantly reinterpret the past in light of an evolving present; we don’t chase a single universal truth. We also (in the ideal at least) write books and articles that are closer to works of philosophy and literature than they are to scientific tracts.
